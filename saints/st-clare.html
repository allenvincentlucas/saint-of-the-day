SAINT OF THE DAY · 13TH CENTURY

St. Clare of Assisi

A wealthy 18-year-old climbed out a side door at night to give away everything she owned, and spent the next forty years convincing the Church that owning nothing was not a problem to be solved.

Feast August 11 Founder, Poor Clares Assisi, Italy · 1193/94–1253

A Palace Girl and a Beggar Preacher

Born Into Everything She Would Give Away

Clare was born in Assisi in either 1193 or 1194 — sources differ on the exact year — the eldest daughter of a noble family with a palace in the city and land beyond it.

Her mother, Ortolana, was known across Assisi for her own piety, having made pilgrimages as far as the Holy Land; her father died while Clare was still young. Clare was raised in comfort, educated, and set apart for the kind of advantageous marriage her family's status expected — but she was already quietly setting food aside from her own table to give to the poor.

Everything changed when she was eighteen. Francis of Assisi, a former wealthy merchant's son turned barefoot preacher, came to speak in the church of San Giorgio during Lent. Clare sought him out afterward for guidance, and what began as spiritual direction became something neither of them had originally planned.

A Door Left Open at Night

Palm Sunday, 1212

With her family arranging a marriage she had no intention of accepting, Clare slipped out of her family's palace on the night of Palm Sunday and made her way, in the dark, to the small Porziuncola chapel where Francis and his brothers gathered.

There, before the altar, Francis cut her hair and clothed her in a plain habit, and Clare made her vows — the first woman to formally join his movement. Her furious family tried to physically drag her back home days later; Clare is said to have clung to the altar and bared her shorn head to prove there was no going back. Within weeks, her younger sister Agnes joined her too, defying the same family a second time.

Francis placed the two women at the small church of San Damiano, which he had earlier rebuilt with his own hands, and it was there that the community later known as the Poor Clares took root — and where Clare would remain, almost without leaving, for the next forty-one years.

Clare's great struggle as abbess was not against outside enemies but against her own Church's discomfort with what she'd chosen. Cardinal Ugolino, later Pope Gregory IX, drafted a rule for her community that allowed the shared ownership of property — standard practice for religious houses, and safer. Clare refused it, insisting on what she called the “privilege of poverty”: that her sisters would own nothing at all, individually or in common, trusting entirely on daily begging by the friars for their food. She spent decades defending that position against well-meaning pressure to soften it.

A Rule Written Two Days Before She Died

What Outlasted Her

Tradition holds that Clare, gravely ill in 1240 and again in 1241, twice carried the Blessed Sacrament to the walls of San Damiano as invading soldiers approached Assisi — and both times, the attackers withdrew.

Whatever the precise historical mechanics, the story cemented her reputation as a protector of her city, and it's why she's sometimes invoked against invasion and for peace. Late in her life, bedridden and too ill to attend Christmas Mass, she reported seeing and hearing the entire liturgy from her cell as though projected onto her wall from the church across town — the basis on which Pope Pius XII named her patron of television in 1958.

Clare spent the final decades of her life fighting for one specific thing: papal approval of a rule guaranteeing her sisters could own absolutely nothing. Pope after pope resisted, worried it was impractical for cloistered women who couldn't beg for themselves the way the friars did. Clare never relented. Pope Innocent IV finally approved her rule — the first religious rule known to have been written by a woman — on 9 August 1253. Clare died two days later, on 11 August, with the document, and the argument it had taken her whole adult life to win, in hand.

A Movement, Not a Single Convent

By the time of her death, the Poor Clares had spread across Italy, France, and Germany, drawing thousands of women, including a princess of Bohemia and a niece of the king of Hungary. Clare was canonized just two years later, in 1255, by Pope Alexander IV. She remains patron of eye disorders, embroiderers, and, thanks to that Christmas vision, television and telecommunications.

Prayer

For Her Intercession

Saint Clare, you looked into the mirror of eternity and found that everything you had been taught to hold onto was worth setting down. Teach us to tell the difference between what we need and what we've merely been told to want, and give us your patience for the long, quiet arguments worth having. Pray for those who follow a call their families don't understand, and for anyone trying to hold onto something the world keeps insisting they simplify. Amen.
