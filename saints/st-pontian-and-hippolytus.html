SAINTS OF THE DAY · 3RD CENTURY

Sts. Pontian & Hippolytus

A pope and the man who set himself up against him as history's first antipope spent eighteen years as rivals — then were exiled to the same mines, and died reconciled.

Feast August 13 Pope & Priest, Rome Died in Exile, Sardinia, 235 A.D.

A Church Split in Two

The Pope and the Antipope

Hippolytus was a Roman priest, and by far the most learned theologian and writer in the Church of his generation — commentaries on Scripture, treatises against heresy, and a record of early Christian liturgical practice all bear his name.

He was also a rigorist. When Pope Callixtus I chose leniency toward Christians who had lapsed or sinned gravely and later sought reconciliation, Hippolytus accused him of dangerous laxity and gathered a breakaway following, having himself elected as a rival bishop of Rome around 217 — history's first antipope. The schism he created outlasted Callixtus, then Pope Urban I, and continued into the reign of Pontian, who became pope in 230.

Pontian, for his part, held a Roman synod that confirmed the condemnation of the theologian Origen for heresy, and led the Church through the schism without managing to resolve it. For years, two men in the same city each claimed to be the legitimate bishop of Rome.

The Mines of Sardinia

What Ended the Schism

In 235, the emperor Maximinus Thrax opened a new persecution aimed squarely at the Church's leadership. Pontian was arrested and sentenced to hard labor in Sardinia's mines — a sentence widely understood, at the time, as death by slower means.

To make sure the Church would not be left without a functioning bishop while he was gone, Pontian resigned the papacy on 28 September 235 — the first pope in history to voluntarily step down from office. A new pope, Anterus, was elected within weeks. Not long after, Hippolytus was arrested in the same persecution and sent to the very same mines.

What happened between the two men there is recorded only in outline, not detail: somewhere in the brutal conditions of Sardinian exile, the eighteen-year schism ended. Sources differ slightly on the exact timing — some place the reconciliation shortly before the two men's exile, others during it — but the tradition is unanimous that it happened, and that it happened before either man died.

Historians still debate exactly how and when the reconciliation took place — whether Hippolytus and his followers formally rejoined the Roman church before the exile, or whether the schism simply dissolved once its two leaders were removed from Rome and sharing the same suffering. What isn't in dispute is the outcome: by the time both men died in the mines later that year, they were remembered by the Church not as rivals, but together.

One Tomb, One Witness

Brought Home Together

Both men died in Sardinia later in 235, worn down by the harsh conditions of the mines. Some years later, Pope Fabian had their remains brought back to Rome and given honorable burial.

Pontian was interred in the papal crypt of the Catacomb of Callixtus — the same crypt that would later hold Pope Sixtus II and several other early bishops of Rome — on 13 August, the date that became his feast. In 1909, archaeologists recovered his original burial inscription in that crypt, confirming the ancient account. Hippolytus, remarkably, was buried nearby and came to be venerated as a saint and martyr in his own right, despite having spent most of his life in schism from the very Church that now honors him.

An Unusual Kind of Sainthood

Few figures in Christian history follow the arc Hippolytus did: brilliant theologian, self-declared rival bishop, reconciled penitent, and finally martyr and saint, all in one lifetime. The Church did not erase his years of schism from the record, nor pretend the division never happened. It simply insisted that the reconciliation mattered more than the rivalry — and paired his feast permanently with the pope he had once opposed.

Prayer

For Their Intercession

Saints Pontian and Hippolytus, you were divided for the better part of two decades over how the Church should treat sinners, and you found your way back to each other only in a mine on a forgotten island. Teach us that no rivalry, however old or however righteous it feels, is worth more than reconciliation. Pray for divided churches, estranged friends, and anyone who has been at odds with someone for so long that peace feels impossible. Amen.
