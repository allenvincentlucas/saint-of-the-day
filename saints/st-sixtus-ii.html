SAINT OF THE DAY · 3RD CENTURY

St. Sixtus II

Arrested mid-Mass in a Roman catacomb and beheaded within the hour — his name has been spoken at the altar at every Mass since.

Feast Aug 7 (Aug 6, Extraordinary Form & Orthodox) Pope, 257–258 A.D. Named in the Roman Canon
01

Rome Under Valerian

A Pope Elected Into Danger

Sixtus became Bishop of Rome on 30–31 August 257. He would hold the office for barely a year.

His origins are genuinely uncertain. The Liber Pontificalis, an early register of popes, calls him a Greek by birth and a former philosopher — but the Catholic Encyclopedia and most modern historians think this likely confuses him with an unrelated Greek Pythagorean writer of the same name. What is better documented is his temperament: his contemporary Pontius, biographer of St. Cyprian of Carthage, called him “a good and peaceful priest.” Early in his pontificate, Sixtus mended a bitter rift between Rome and the African and Eastern churches over whether converts baptized by heretics needed to be rebaptized — choosing conciliation over his predecessor Stephen I's harder line, without abandoning Rome's own practice.

He did not have long to lead in peace. Soon after his election, the emperor Valerian issued an edict against Christian assembly. When that failed to end the faith, a second, harsher edict followed in 258: bishops, priests, and deacons who persisted were to be executed on discovery. Rome's Christians kept worshipping anyway — in the catacombs, the underground burial tunnels cut into the soft volcanic rock outside the city walls, changing the time and place of their gatherings to stay ahead of the authorities.

02

The Cemetery, the Chair, the Sword

Caught Mid-Homily

On 6 August 258, soldiers found Sixtus exactly where the edict said they would: leading his people in worship, underground.

Accounts agree on the shape of the story even where they differ on its exact setting. Sixtus had taken to holding services in a cemetery along the Appian Way — some sources name the catacomb of Callixtus, others the nearby cemetery of Praetextatus, which was watched less closely by the authorities. He was seated in his bishop's chair, addressing the community gathered around him, when soldiers broke in. He was arrested along with the deacons attending him. Sources also differ on how many died with him that day: the earliest surviving account, a letter from St. Cyprian of Carthage, names four deacons — Januarius, Vincent, Magnus, and Stephen; later tradition adds two more, Felicissimus and Agapitus, executed the same day though not at Sixtus's side.

ROMAN MARTYROLOGY, ENTRY FOR AUGUST 7
“At Rome, on the Appian Way, in the cemetery of Callistus, the birthday of blessed Xystus, Pope and Martyr, who, together with his deacons, was seized in the very act of offering the Sacrifice, in the persecution of Valerian, and obtained the crown of martyrdom.”
Roman Martyrology, traditional English rendering (public domain liturgical text)

What happened next became one of the more quietly told stories of the early Church. Sixtus's chief deacon, Lawrence, is said to have run to his side and asked why his bishop would go to death without him. Sixtus is remembered as telling him he would follow in a few days, with an even greater trial to come. Lawrence was martyred four days later, on 10 August — roasted, according to a persistent but historically doubtful later legend, on a gridiron. Sixtus himself was beheaded that same day in August 258, either on the spot in the cemetery chapel or led back to the place of his arrest to die there; accounts differ on this detail too, a reminder of how much of this story survives only in fragments passed down through Cyprian's letters and later Roman tradition rather than a single clean record.

Because the primary sources for this martyrdom are a handful of ancient letters and later liturgical tradition rather than an eyewitness narrative, historians hold several of its details — the exact cemetery, the precise number of companions, whether Sixtus was killed where he sat or elsewhere — as probable rather than certain. The core fact is not in dispute: a pope was executed for the crime of leading Christian worship, within a year of taking office.

03

A Name Cut Into Every Mass

Why He Is Still Named Today

Sixtus was buried nearby, in what became known as the Crypt of the Popes, in the Catacomb of Callixtus — a chamber that would go on to hold several of Rome's early bishops.

A century later, Pope Damasus I — who had a particular devotion to Rome's early martyrs and commissioned inscriptions for many of their tombs — placed an epitaph over Sixtus's grave honoring his courage in offering himself first rather than let the soldiers harm his flock. That tomb, and the Crypt of the Popes around it, still exist beneath Rome today and remain a site of pilgrimage.

Spoken, Not Just Remembered

Sixtus's most unusual legacy isn't a shrine or a patronage — it's a sentence. His name was written into the Roman Canon, the oldest Eucharistic Prayer of the Latin Mass, sometime before the end of the seventh century, listed among the martyrs commemorated at the altar. Wherever that prayer is used today, his name is still spoken aloud at Mass — not a memory of him, but a use of him, over seventeen centuries after his death.

Unlike many early martyrs, Sixtus was never attached to a major patronage — no trade, illness, or cause claims him as protector, beyond a local devotion in the Italian town of Bellegra. His significance instead sits in what he did with the last year of his life: he kept a divided Church talking to itself, and then kept it gathered for the Eucharist even after that gathering was made a capital offense. The current General Roman Calendar moved his feast from 6 August (the day of his death, since given over to the Transfiguration) to 7 August in 1969, where it remains an optional memorial shared with St. Cajetan.

05

Prayer

For His Intercession

Saint Sixtus, you were seated among your people, teaching them, when the sword came for you, and you did not run. Ask for us the courage to keep gathering for the Eucharist even when it costs us something, and the peace to trust that a divided Church can still be healed by patient, conciliatory love. Pray for popes and bishops who lead in dangerous times, and for all who are asked to choose between silence and the altar. Amen.
