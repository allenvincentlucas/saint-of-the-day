SAINT OF THE DAY · 20TH CENTURY

St. Maximilian Kolbe

A Polish friar who built a media empire to spread devotion to Mary sheltered thousands of refugees from the Nazis, was sent to Auschwitz, and volunteered to die in another prisoner's place.

Feast August 14 Franciscan Friar & Martyr Zduńska Wola, Poland · 1894–1941

Two Crowns, Chosen Young

The Choice He Said He Made at Twelve

Rajmund Kolbe was born on 8 January 1894 in Zduńska Wola, then part of the Russian Empire, to a poor weaver and a midwife.

Both his parents would later enter religious life themselves — his mother as a Benedictine sister, his father as a Franciscan. His father was hanged by the Russians in 1914 for fighting in the Polish independence movement. As a boy, Kolbe later recounted, he prayed before a statue of Mary asking what would become of him, and believed she appeared to him holding two crowns: one white, for purity, and one red, for martyrdom, asking which he would accept. He said he chose both.

At sixteen he took the religious name Maximilian on entering the Conventual Franciscans, and was ordained a priest in Rome in 1918, despite lifelong poor health from tuberculosis that would trouble him the rest of his life.

A City Built Around a Printing Press

Fighting a War With Newspapers

In 1917 Kolbe founded the Militia Immaculatae, a movement of consecration to Mary, and set out to spread it as widely as modern technology allowed.

He built Niepokalanów — “City of the Immaculate” — outside Warsaw in 1927, which grew into the largest Franciscan community in the world, home to over 700 friars running printing presses and eventually a radio station. His magazine, Knight of the Immaculata, reached a circulation over a million copies. In 1930, still in poor health, he traveled to Japan and founded a sister community outside Nagasaki, which would later survive the atomic bombing, shielded by the geography of a nearby mountain.

When Nazi Germany invaded Poland in 1939, Niepokalanów became a shelter for an estimated 2,000–3,000 refugees, most of them Jewish, even as Kolbe's presses kept printing material critical of the occupation. He was arrested and briefly held in 1939, released, then arrested again in February 1941 for sheltering refugees and aiding the Polish resistance. He was sent to Auschwitz that May, prisoner number 16670.

The Man He Stepped Forward For

“I Want to Take His Place”

On 31 July 1941, a prisoner from Kolbe's barracks went missing. Camp rules called for ten men, chosen at random, to be starved to death as a warning to the rest.

As the commandant walked the assembled lines pointing out victims, one of the chosen men, Franciszek Gajowniczek, a Polish army sergeant, cried out for the wife and children he would never see again. Kolbe stepped out of formation. When the astonished commandant demanded to know who he was, survivor testimony records his answer simply: he was a Catholic priest, and he wanted to take that man's place. The request was granted.

“I am a Catholic priest. I want to take his place.”
Widely attested account of Kolbe's words to the camp commandant

Kolbe and the nine others were locked in an underground starvation cell in Block 11. Prisoners who could hear through the walls later described prayer and hymn-singing coming from inside, rather than the despair the guards expected. After two weeks without food or water, Kolbe was one of only four still alive; the camp, needing the cell, ended his life with a lethal injection on 14 August 1941. He was 47. His body was cremated the next day — the feast of the Assumption of Mary.

Kolbe's canonization prompted real theological debate. Traditional Christian martyrdom requires dying in odium fidei — killed specifically out of hatred for the faith — and Kolbe was not selected for the starvation bunker because he was a priest; he volunteered for it. Pope John Paul II resolved the question in 1982 by canonizing him as a “martyr of charity” rather than a martyr in the traditional technical sense — a recognized, if unusual, expansion of how the Church understands giving one's life for others.

The Man Whose Life He Saved

Franciszek Gajowniczek survived Auschwitz and the war, though he returned home to learn his sons had been killed. He spent the rest of his life telling Kolbe's story, and was present at both Kolbe's beatification in 1971 and his canonization in 1982. He died in 1995, fifty-four years after the day Kolbe took his place.

Prayer

For His Intercession

Saint Maximilian Kolbe, you stepped out of a line of condemned men for a stranger you had never met, because he cried out for his family. Teach us that love is not an abstraction — it's a decision made in a single moment, with everything on the line. Pray for prisoners, for refugees seeking shelter, and for anyone weighing whether their own safety matters more than someone else's need. Amen.
